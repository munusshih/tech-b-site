### Design Has Always Been Technical

Technology A and Technology B are two parts of the same design curriculum. Being a designer has actually historically been a very technical job: You had people designing type, cutting type, setting type, operating presses, preparing pages for printing, and all of these jobs required very specific technical knowledge. So the idea was not just that you were making something look good; you actually had to understand how the thing was being produced. And a lot of early modern design education, especially schools like the Bauhaus, was very much connected to workshops, materials, craft, industrial production, and vocational training. So in some ways, having technology classes in design school is not really a new idea at all. It’s just that what we mean by “technology” keeps changing.

Printers understood type, paper, ink, registration, and binding; [desktop publishing](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/personal-computers/17/306) moved production onto personal computers; and [creative coding](https://processing.org/tutorials/overview/) turned the computer into a medium that can move, respond, work with data, and generate variation. The tools change, but designers still need to understand how things are made.

### A Change of Scale

- In the [Tech A](https://tech-a.designfuture.space/), you write code yourself, prototype interactions, and see how inputs, outputs, variables, and rules produce behavior. The goal is not to become a software engineer; it is to learn the language of computation so that you can design with it. You learn to read and write code, and to understand how a system works from the inside.
- In **Technology B**, we still make and prototype, but we also investigate systems too large for one designer to rebuild: platforms, networks, automation, data, AI, infrastructure, and the labor behind them. W

Technology B is not “theory after skills.” It moves between using a system and asking who built it, what it values, what it hides, who benefits, and who carries its costs. A generative-AI text box may look simple while concealing models, datasets, servers, energy, labor, companies, laws, and design decisions. The [official Tech B course site](https://tech-b.designfuture.space/) gives the semester structure, while the [Processing Foundation](https://processingfoundation.org/) supports the creative-coding community that helped establish code as a design medium.

In short: **Tech A asks how to construct a computational system; Tech B asks how to understand, work within, and redesign a technological system bigger than any one of us.**

## What is intelligence?

“AI” is currently used as a promise, a threat, and a label for very different technologies. Before asking what makes intelligence _artificial_, we need to ask what we are calling _intelligence_. Is it learning, adaptation, language, problem-solving, perception, social relation, or meaning-making? There is no single settled definition—the collection assembled by [Shane Legg and Marcus Hutter](https://arxiv.org/abs/0706.3639) shows how differently researchers frame it.

For designers, this is not only a philosophical question. Once a definition becomes categories, measurements, datasets, or interface behavior, it becomes a design decision. A system that recognizes intelligence through speed, accuracy, or fluent language will notice some capacities and ignore others. Our definition determines what a system values, who or what it can recognize, and what it excludes.

### Build a Definition

Define intelligence without using the words _smart_ or _intelligent_. Which qualities does your definition require?

- Learning from experience
- Adapting to an environment
- Solving problems or pursuing goals
- Using language and symbols
- Perceiving through a body
- Relating to other people or living things
- Remembering, imagining, or making meaning

Test your definition against a person, an animal, a plant, a group of people, a thermostat, and a large language model. What does it include? What does it exclude? What evidence would convince you that each example is intelligent?

### The Imitation Game

In [_Computing Machinery and Intelligence_](https://academic.oup.com/mind/article/LIX/236/433/986238) (1950), Alan Turing steps away from the difficult question “Can machines think?” and proposes an observable test he calls the imitation game. Instead of proving what happens inside a machine, an evaluator judges it through written interaction. This move is useful, but it also narrows intelligence to a particular performance: producing language another person finds convincing. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s Turing Test entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/turing-test/) provides additional context.

In Technology B, we will examine how intelligence has been modeled, simulated, and represented across different historical and cultural contexts. We will treat contemporary AI not as a magical or autonomous intelligence, but as a designed structure that emerges from datasets, rules, interfaces, infrastructure, labor, and human decisions.

The [UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Students](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000391105) organizes AI literacy as a progression from **Understand → Apply → Create**. ### What Counts as Technology?

The word technology can get a little bit messy. A pencil sharpener, a pencil, paper, a chair, and a stapler are all technologies; technology does not have to be digital, electronic, or computational. But the fact that almost everything can be called technology does not mean everything is equally relevant to what we are studying. If you want to research a pencil sharpener, my question will be: **What does the pencil sharpener help you understand?** Can you use it to think about automation, standardization, labor, mechanical systems, industrial production, or interfaces? Can you connect that small thing to a larger technological system?

### How We Will Work

- Read a system closely: its rules, inputs, outputs, interfaces, histories, and omissions.
- Prototype with it so that our critique is grounded in direct experience.
- Document the choices, failures, and discoveries that happen while making.
- Ask who built the system, whose knowledge it privileges, who benefits, and who carries its costs.
- Use critique to redirect, redesign, misuse, or refuse a system—not only to make it more efficient.

## The Chinese Room

In [_Minds, Brains, and Programs_](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/minds-brains-and-programs/DC644B47A4299C637C89772FACC2706A) (1980), philosopher John Searle asks us to imagine a person in a room following a rulebook to transform unfamiliar Chinese symbols into convincing replies. From outside the room, the responses appear fluent. Inside, the person does not understand Chinese; they only manipulate symbols according to formal rules.

The thought experiment separates **syntax**—following rules for arranging symbols—from **semantics**—understanding what those symbols mean. Searle uses the distinction to challenge the claim that executing the right program is, by itself, sufficient for understanding. The argument has many replies and counterarguments; the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s Chinese Room entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-room/) is a useful guide to that debate.

### In-class Thought Experiment

1. One person is the **room** and cannot see the larger conversation.
2. One person is the **rulebook**, supplying exact operations without explaining their meaning.
3. One group creates the **inputs** and another group evaluates the **outputs**.
4. Run the exchange, then ask where—if anywhere—understanding exists in the whole system.

### Discussion Prompts

- Can a system that manipulates symbols according to rules be said to understand anything?
- Does the person need to understand, or could the room as a complete system understand?
- Does embodiment, perception, or the ability to act in the world change your answer?
- When a chatbot produces a convincing response, what evidence of understanding do we actually have?

## What is AI?

The term _artificial intelligence_ was used in the [1955 proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence](https://ojs.aaai.org/aimagazine/index.php/aimagazine/article/view/1904/0). The proposal imagined that features of learning and intelligence could be described precisely enough for machines to simulate them. That premise turns intelligence into a design problem: decide which qualities matter, formalize them as rules or representations, and build a system that performs them.

### A Working Definition

For this course, _AI_ can refer to several related but different things:

- A **field of research** concerned with building systems that perform capacities associated with intelligence.
- A **set of techniques**, including classification, search, optimization, machine learning, and generative modeling.
- A **product or interface** through which people encounter those techniques.
- An **infrastructure** of data, models, servers, energy, human labor, institutions, laws, and business decisions.

The [NIST glossary definition of artificial intelligence](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/artificial_intelligence) is useful as a current operational reference, while the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s AI entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/artificial-intelligence/) compares definitions based on thinking versus acting and human performance versus ideal rationality. Whenever someone says “AI,” ask which meaning they are using and what gets hidden when those layers are collapsed into one word.

### ELIZA and the Appearance of Understanding

[ELIZA](https://www.masswerk.at/elizabot/) was created by Joseph Weizenbaum in the 1960s. Its best-known script, DOCTOR, transforms parts of a user’s input into questions that resemble a nondirective therapist’s responses. The program is structurally simple, yet people can quickly project empathy and understanding onto its output. Read [Weizenbaum’s original paper](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/365153.365168), try a [working ELIZA reconstruction](https://www.masswerk.at/elizabot/), and explore the archival project [Finding ELIZA](https://findingeliza.org/).

#### Variation Pattern

```js title="Variation Pattern"
function draw() {
  background(250, 245, 236);
  noStroke();

  for (let i = 0; i < 40; i += 1) {
    const x = random(width);
    const y = random(height);
    const d = random(8, 40);
    fill(20, 30, 60, 120);
    circle(x, y, d);
  }
}
```
